This is not an online store.

I restore and modify original Xbox consoles for people who plan to actually use them. There is no shopping cart because there is no single standard Xbox build sitting on a shelf with one fixed price.

Every console starts with a different donor, and every customer has a different display, network, storage plan, controller setup, and reason for owning one. Those details matter more than most people expect.

The short version: before I can tell you what an Xbox should cost, I need to know what you actually need the Xbox to do and what you plan to connect it to.

This tour explains the parts people usually do not know to ask about. It is intentionally broken into short sections so you can deal with one subject at a time.

Step 1

Why there is an application.

I am not trying to make buying an Xbox difficult for the sake of it. The application is there because the wrong build for your setup wastes your money and creates support problems that could have been avoided before I ever shipped anything.

What I need to understand

  • What display you plan to use and what inputs it actually has
  • Whether the Xbox will be wired to your network and how
  • Whether you want Insignia and online play
  • How much storage you realistically need
  • Whether you want OEM or modern controllers
  • How comfortable you are troubleshooting old hardware

What that lets me do

  • Recommend the build that fits your use instead of the most expensive one
  • Catch display or networking problems before they become your problem
  • Avoid selling you hardware or accessories you do not need
  • Explain the limitations before money changes hands
  • Know what needs to be tested before the console leaves me
Step 2

“I only want to play Rainbow Six” does not make the Xbox cheap.

This is one of the most common misunderstandings. The number of games you want changes how much storage makes sense. It does not remove the work required to make a twenty-plus-year-old console reliable.

If you only want a few games...What does not disappear
A smaller hard drive may be enough.The motherboard still has to be inspected, cleaned, serviced, and tested.
You may need fewer apps or extras.The console still needs a reliable storage setup, dashboard, EEPROM backup, and configuration.
You may not need every accessory.Your TV still needs a compatible video path and online play still needs a workable network.
Your final build can be simpler.The donor console, repair parts, labor, testing, and shipping still exist.
A small game library can reduce the size of the build. It does not turn restoration work into a $40 used-console transaction.
Step 3

What actually happens to a console before it leaves.

“Modded Xbox” describes the software people see. Most of the work is everything underneath it.

Hardware and preservation

  • Full teardown, cleaning, and board inspection
  • Clock-capacitor service appropriate to the motherboard revision
  • Known problem capacitors inspected and replaced when required
  • Fresh thermal interface material and cooling inspection
  • DVD, controller ports, video output, and power behavior checked
  • EEPROM captured and preserved before storage work

Storage, software, and testing

  • Hard-drive upgrade and lock/configuration matched to the console
  • Dashboard, applications, title updates, and file structure configured
  • Insignia and network setup tested when part of the build
  • Controllers and ports checked under real use
  • Extended stress testing before final clean and packing
  • Build information and EEPROM recovery material retained

Some of that is bench work. Some of it is file transfer time. Some of it is simply running the machine long enough to learn whether it can be trusted.

About the DVD drive: even when it works, it is aging mechanical hardware. The normal day-to-day plan is to run installed games from the hard drive and avoid putting unnecessary hours on the optical drive.
Step 4

An analog console in a digital house.

The original Xbox was designed around analog video. A modern HDMI-only television was not part of the design brief. Connecting the two means converting signals, and the quality of that conversion matters.

The console side

  • Composite is the lowest-quality native video path.
  • Component supports the progressive/high-definition modes people usually want.
  • The Xbox does not output HDMI natively.
  • CRT and older HDTV setups can behave very differently from modern panels.

The modern-display side

  • An HDMI-only display needs a proper analog-to-digital adapter.
  • Cheap converters often start with the worst source signal and add more problems.
  • 480p-era art can look harsh when scaled across a large 4K panel.
  • Your display choice is part of the Xbox setup, not an unrelated detail.
For HDMI-only setups I generally recommend the Electron Shepherd XOUT because it uses the right signal path and handles the job without turning the display chain into another project.

Step 5

Networking is still 2001 networking.

The original Xbox has wired Ethernet. It does not have built-in Wi-Fi. If you want online play through Insignia, I need to know how the console is getting to your router and whether you can actually manage that router if something needs to change.

The easy case

Direct Ethernet from the Xbox to a normal router or switch. This is the setup I can reason about, reproduce, and meaningfully help troubleshoot.

The complicated case

ISP combo boxes, wireless bridges, powerline adapters, unusual firewall rules, or networks nobody has admin access to. These can work, but every extra layer creates another place for old hardware to disagree with modern infrastructure.

Your internet plan can be extremely fast and the Xbox can still have a networking problem. Bandwidth and compatibility are not the same thing.

Read the full networking section.

Step 6

Controllers and adapters are part of the system.

Original Xbox controller ports are electrically related to USB, but a modern USB or Bluetooth controller does not become an Xbox controller because the plug can be adapted.

OEM controller

The simplest option. It speaks the protocol the console expects and adds no translation layer.

Modern controller

A device such as an OGX-Mini translates modern controller protocols into what the Xbox understands. That adds capability, but it is another piece of hardware that needs to be chosen and configured correctly.

The same general rule applies to video adapters, network bridges, and other accessories: compatibility is a system-level question. The Xbox is only one part of the chain.

Learn about the OGX-Mini builds.

Step 7

Why there is no one answer to “How much?”

Price follows the actual build. I can estimate ranges, but I cannot responsibly quote one universal number before I know what I am building and what the donor console requires.

Things that change the cost

  • Condition and revision of the donor console
  • Capacitors, DVD drive, or other hardware that needs service
  • Hard-drive size and storage hardware
  • Video and controller adapters required by your setup
  • Special repair or recovery work
  • Shipping and packing requirements

What I do not want to sell you

  • A bigger drive just because a bigger number sounds better
  • A modchip when the build does not need one
  • An HDMI adapter that does not fit your display plan
  • Accessories you will never use
  • A console I already know is a bad match for your setup
The goal is not the cheapest Xbox or the most expensive Xbox. It is the correct Xbox for the person who is going to own it.
Step 8

What I stand behind, and what ownership means.

I want the console to arrive working, documented, and tested for the setup we agreed on. I also want the owner to understand that this is modified twenty-plus-year-old hardware, not a sealed modern appliance.

My responsibility

  • Describe the build honestly
  • Repair and configure what I say I repaired and configured
  • Test the machine before it leaves
  • Keep recovery information that matters
  • Help diagnose a real problem instead of immediately blaming the customer

Your responsibility

  • Use the display, adapters, and network setup we discussed
  • Read the documentation before changing settings
  • Ask before changing something you do not understand
  • Be able to explain what changed if a problem starts after you changed something
  • Accept that old hardware still has old-hardware limitations

If you would rather understand every part of the machine yourself, I genuinely encourage learning to mod your own Xbox. I would rather help someone learn than sell them something they are not comfortable owning.

Electron Shepherd XOUT // Why it is the right adapter

The XOUT works well because it starts with the proper component signal, keeps the analog path short, and converts to digital without adding unnecessary complexity.

That matters because most cheap HDMI adapters are cheap by cutting the wrong corners: bad shielding, weak components, sloppy signal handling, stereo-only audio, and inconsistent results from one TV to the next.

The XOUT works across Xbox revisions, passes Dolby Digital, and solves the problem without turning your setup into an experiment. It is not magic. It is just the adapter that does the job the way it should have been done in the first place.

OGX-Mini // What it is

The OGX-Mini is an open-source controller adapter project for the original Xbox. It is not a retail product. There is no single official version — different builders produce it with different boards, different firmware, and different levels of quality control. That matters when you are shopping around.

The version I build is the DVD dongle style — it sits in the DVD drive bay area rather than hanging off the front controller ports. Clean install, no mess, no cable dangling out of the front of the console.

What it does: it translates between modern wireless controllers and the original Xbox's proprietary controller protocol. That translation introduces a small amount of latency — same as any protocol conversion. In practice you will not feel it. But it is not magic and anyone telling you otherwise is selling you something.

Not every version of the OGX-Mini supports every modern controller. Some builds have better firmware, better controller compatibility, and better reliability than others. If you are buying one from somewhere else, check what controllers are actually supported on that specific build before you commit.

If you want modern wireless controller support on original hardware done right, this is the answer. If you want zero additional complexity, use an OEM Xbox controller and call it a day.
